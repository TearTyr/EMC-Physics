Topic 1 of 4

Electric charges & electric fields

Charge is the property of matter that makes it feel the electromagnetic force. Charges create electric fields in the space around them, and those fields push on every other charge. Everything in this module — circuits, motors, generators — follows from that one idea.

Learning objectives

  • State Coulomb’s law and calculate the force between two point charges.
  • Predict whether a force is attractive or repulsive from the signs of the charges.
  • Define the electric field as force per unit charge and give its units.
  • Draw and interpret electric field lines for single charges, dipoles and like charges.
  • Apply superposition to find the net field of several charges.

1.1 · Positive and negative charge

Matter contains two kinds of charge, named positive and negative by Benjamin Franklin. The rule that governs everything is simple:

The fundamental rule

Like charges repel. Unlike charges attract.

Charge is carried by particles. The proton carries +e = +1.602×10−19 C and the electron carries −e. Charge is quantised — every observable charge is an integer multiple of e — and it is conserved: rubbing a glass rod with silk does not create charge, it moves electrons from the glass to the silk, leaving equal and opposite amounts behind.

Conductor
A material with charges free to move (metals, salt water, your body). Excess charge on a conductor spreads out over its surface.
Insulator
A material whose charges are bound in place (rubber, glass, dry air, plastic). Charge stays where you put it.
Charging by friction
Contact plus rubbing transfers electrons; the material higher on the triboelectric series loses them and ends up positive.
Charging by induction
Bringing a charged object near a conductor redistributes its charges without touching it. Earth the conductor and the redistribution becomes permanent.
Real world

The spark you get from a door handle after walking on carpet is a few thousand volts driving charge across a millimetre of air. Lightning is the same process at kilometre scale: collisions between ice particles separate charge inside a storm cloud until the field exceeds about 3×106 V/m, the breakdown strength of air.

1.2 · Coulomb’s law

In 1785 Charles-Augustin de Coulomb measured the force between small charged spheres with a torsion balance and found an inverse-square law that looks exactly like Newton’s law of gravitation — but roughly 1036 times stronger, and able to repel as well as attract.

F = k · |q1 q2| / r2 k = 1/(4πε0) = 8.988×109 N·m2/C2 F in newtons · q in coulombs · r = centre-to-centre separation in metres

Three things to notice about this equation:

  1. It is an inverse-square law. Double r and F drops to a quarter; triple r and it drops to a ninth.
  2. The magnitude uses |q1q2|, so F is never negative. The signs of the charges only tell you the direction: like signs repel, unlike signs attract.
  3. The two forces are a Newton’s-third-law pair. The force q1 exerts on q2 is equal in magnitude and opposite in direction to the force q2 exerts on q1 — even if one charge is a thousand times bigger.
Worked example

Charges q1 = +3 µC and q2 = −2 µC sit 0.30 m apart in air. Find the force.

F = (8.988×109)(3×10−6)(2×10−6) / (0.30)2
  = (8.988×109)(6×10−12) / 0.09 = 5.39×10−2 / 0.09 = 0.60 N

The signs are opposite, so the force is attractive: each charge is pulled toward the other with 0.60 N. Notice how large that is — 0.60 N is the weight of a 60 g apple, produced by charges you could never hold in your hand.

Common mistake

Coulomb’s law in this form applies to point charges (or to uniform spheres, where r is measured centre to centre). For an extended, irregular charged object you must integrate over its charge distribution — that is where calculus enters this topic.

Superposition

With three or more charges, each pair interacts independently. The net force on any one charge is the vector sum of the individual forces:

Fnet = F1 + F2 + F3 + …add as vectors (components), not as magnitudes

1.3 · The electric field and its direction

Rather than thinking of charges reaching across empty space, physics says each charge modifies the space around it, creating an electric field E. A second charge then feels a force because of the field at its own location. The field is defined operationally by the force on a small positive test charge q0:

E = F / q0 point charge:  E = k·q / r2 Units: N/C, which is identical to V/m
The direction rule

E points in the direction a positive test charge would be pushed. So the field of a positive charge points radially outward, and the field of a negative charge points radially inward.

Field-line rules

  • Lines start on positive charge and end on negative charge (or go off to infinity).
  • Lines never cross — if they did, the field at that point would have two directions.
  • The density of lines represents field strength: closer together means stronger.
  • The tangent to a line at any point is the direction of E there.
  • The number of lines leaving a charge is proportional to |q|, which is why a +2q charge gets twice as many lines as a +q charge.
Worked example — field of a point charge

Find E at a point 0.20 m from a +5 µC charge, and the force on an electron placed there.

E = kq/r2 = (8.988×109)(5×10−6)/(0.20)2 = 4.49×104 / 0.04 = 1.12×106 N/C, directed away from the charge.
F = qE = (1.602×10−19)(1.12×106) = 1.80×10−13 N — toward the charge, because the electron is negative.

The uniform field: parallel plates

Between two large oppositely charged parallel plates the field is essentially uniform — same magnitude and direction everywhere — which makes it the workhorse of electrostatics:

E = V / dV = potential difference between the plates, d = their separation

A 5000 V supply across plates 2 cm apart gives E = 5000/0.02 = 2.5×105 V/m. Inside a uniform field a charge experiences a constant force, so its motion is exactly the projectile motion you already know — with qE/m in place of g.

Real world

Laser printers and photocopiers steer charged toner with electric fields. Inkjet printers charge each droplet and deflect it between charged plates. Electrostatic precipitators in power stations charge smoke particles and collect them on plates, removing over 99% of the ash. The cathode-ray tube in an old television is a pure demonstration of F = qE deflecting electrons.

Three misconceptions to avoid

1. “A bigger charge feels a bigger force, so it accelerates more.” No — a = F/m; the field is a property of space, independent of the test charge.

2. “Field lines are the paths charges follow.” They are not. A charge released in a curved field moves along a trajectory determined by its velocity and the force, which generally is not the field line.

3. “E = 0 means no charge is there.” It means the vector sum cancels — between two like charges there is a null point where the fields are equal and opposite.

1.6 · Check your understanding

Think first, then click to reveal the answer.

Two identical spheres carry +6 µC and −2 µC. They are touched together and then separated. What does each carry afterwards?
+2 µC each. Charge is conserved and, because the spheres are identical, it shares equally: (6 − 2)/2 = +2 µC. The force between them afterwards is repulsive.
The separation between two point charges is tripled. By what factor does the force change?
It becomes 1/9 of the original. F is proportional to 1/r2, so 32 = 9 in the denominator.
Can an electric field line ever form a closed loop?
No — not for electrostatic fields. Field lines begin on positive charge and end on negative charge. (Magnetic field lines do form closed loops, because there are no magnetic monopoles — that contrast is the subject of Topic 3.)
An electron is released from rest in a uniform field of 2.0×104 N/C pointing to the right. Which way does it accelerate, and how fast?
To the left, at 3.5×1015 m/s2. F = |q|E = (1.602×10−19)(2.0×104) = 3.2×10−15 N, opposite to E because the charge is negative. a = F/m = 3.2×10−15 / 9.109×10−31 = 3.5×1015 m/s2.
Why does a charged balloon stick to a neutral wall?
Polarisation (charging by induction). The balloon’s charge pushes like charges in the wall’s surface atoms slightly away and pulls opposite charges slightly closer. The nearer opposite charge wins because the field is stronger at shorter distance, giving a net attraction.
