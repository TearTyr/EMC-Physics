Electromagnetic induction

In 1831 Michael Faraday discovered that a changing magnetic field creates an electric field — and therefore a voltage in a wire. That single discovery is the reason almost every joule of electrical energy on Earth exists: generators, transformers, wireless chargers, induction hobs and guitar pickups all run on it.

Learning objectives

  • Define magnetic flux Φ = BA cosθ and state its unit.
  • Apply Faraday’s law EMF = −N·dΦ/dt to calculate an induced voltage.
  • List the three ways to change the flux through a coil.
  • Use Lenz’s law to predict the direction of an induced current.
  • Explain Lenz’s law as a statement of conservation of energy.

4.1 · Magnetic flux

Flux measures how much magnetic field threads a loop. It depends on the field strength, the area of the loop, and the orientation between them:

Φ = B · A · cosθ general form: Φ = ∫ B · dA Φ in webers (Wb) = T·m2 · θ is the angle between B and the normal to the loop

A loop of area 0.02 m2 in a 0.5 T field has Φ = 0.01 Wb when the field is perpendicular to its plane, and zero when the field lies in the plane — even though the field is just as strong. Rotate the loop and the flux changes.

4.2 · Faraday’s law

EMF = − N · dΦ/dt N = number of turns · EMF in volts · Φ in webers · t in seconds

The induced EMF is proportional to the rate of change of flux linkage — not to the flux itself. A huge but constant flux induces nothing at all. There are exactly three ways to change Φ = BA cosθ:

  1. Change B — move a magnet toward or away from the coil, or vary the current in a nearby electromagnet.
  2. Change A — stretch, squash or slide the loop so the enclosed area changes.
  3. Change θ — rotate the coil in the field. This is what every generator does.
Worked example 1 — changing B

A 200-turn coil of area 0.010 m2 sits perpendicular to a field that rises from 0 to 0.5 T in 0.20 s.

dΦ/dt = A·dB/dt = 0.010 × (0.5/0.20) = 0.025 Wb/s
EMF = N·dΦ/dt = 200 × 0.025 = 5.0 V

Worked example 2 — rotating a coil (an AC generator)

A coil of N turns and area A rotates at angular frequency ω in a field B, so θ = ωt:

Φ = BA cos(ωt)  ⇒  EMF = −N dΦ/dt = NBAω sin(ωt)
peak EMF = NBAω. With N = 100, B = 0.5 T, A = 0.02 m2, f = 50 Hz (ω = 314 rad/s):
peak = 100 × 0.5 × 0.02 × 314 = 314 V

That is a mains-sized voltage, produced by a hand-sized coil spinning 50 times a second. The output is sinusoidal and alternating — which is why the grid carries AC.

Motional EMF

A rod of length L sliding at speed v perpendicular to a field B has its free charges deflected by F = qvB until the electric field they set up balances the magnetic one. The result is a voltage across the rod:

EMF = B L va 0.20 m rod at 3 m/s in 0.5 T gives 0.30 V

If the rod completes a circuit of resistance R, the current is I = BLv/R and the magnetic force on that current is F = BIL = B2L2v/R, opposing the motion. The mechanical power you supply, Fv = B2L2v2/R, is exactly the electrical power I2R dissipated in the resistor — energy conservation is built into the equations.

4.3 · Lenz’s law and the minus sign

The negative sign in Faraday’s law is not a formality; it is a complete physical statement known as Lenz’s law:

Lenz’s law

The induced current flows in such a direction that its own magnetic field opposes the change in flux that produced it.

Note carefully: it opposes the change, not the flux itself.

Why Lenz’s law must be true

Suppose the induced current helped the change instead of opposing it. The magnet would accelerate, increasing the flux faster, driving a bigger current, which would pull the magnet harder still — energy from nowhere. Lenz’s law is conservation of energy applied to induction: the electrical energy dissipated as I2R heat is paid for by the mechanical work you do against the magnetic opposition. If you stop pushing, the current stops immediately.

The three-step recipe for finding a direction

1. Is the flux through the loop increasing or decreasing? 2. The induced field must oppose that change — so it points opposite to B if the flux is rising, and along B if it is falling. 3. Curl your right-hand fingers around the loop in the current direction; your thumb then points along the induced field. That fixes the current direction.

Real world

Generators rotate a coil in a field (change θ) — a 1 GW turbine is Faraday’s law at industrial scale. Transformers use a changing current in the primary to create a changing flux in an iron core, inducing a voltage in the secondary; the turns ratio sets the voltage ratio, which is why the grid can transmit at 400 kV and deliver at 230 V.

Induction hobs drive 20–50 kHz AC through a coil, inducing eddy currents that heat the pan directly — the glass stays cool. Wireless phone chargers are small transformers with an air gap. Eddy-current brakes slow trains and fairground drop towers with no contact and no wear. Guitar pickups have a magnet inside a coil; a vibrating steel string changes the flux and generates the signal. Airport security and metal detectors look for the eddy currents induced in metal objects.

The magnet-through-coil experiment changes flux by moving B. A power station changes flux by rotating θ: a coil of N turns and area A spins at frequency f in a steady field, so Φ = NBA·cos(2πft) and therefore

EMF = N·B·A·(2πf)·sin(2πft) a pure sine wave — this is why the mains is alternating current

4.6 · Check your understanding

A 500-turn coil of area 4.0×10−3 m2 is perpendicular to a field that falls from 0.80 T to 0.20 T in 0.10 s. Find the induced EMF.
12 V. dΦ/dt = A·dB/dt = 4.0×10−3 × (0.60/0.10) = 0.024 Wb/s. EMF = N·dΦ/dt = 500 × 0.024 = 12 V.
A magnet is held stationary inside a coil connected to a galvanometer. What does the meter read, and why?
Zero. The flux is large but constant, so dΦ/dt = 0 and EMF = −N·dΦ/dt = 0. Induction requires change. This is why generators must turn and why transformers only work on AC.
You push a magnet into a coil twice as fast. What happens to the EMF, the current and the energy dissipated?
EMF doubles (dΦ/dt doubles), current doubles (I = EMF/R), and power quadruples (P = EMF2/R). The total energy per pass stays the same because the pass takes half as long — but you had to push twice as hard, which is exactly Lenz’s law demanding payment.
Why does the EMF trace cross zero as the magnet passes through the centre of the coil?
At the centre the flux is at its maximum, and at a maximum the derivative is zero. The flux stops rising and starts falling, so dΦ/dt — and therefore the EMF — passes through zero and reverses sign. The trace has two opposite peaks, one on the way in and one on the way out.
A copper ring is held above a solenoid. When the solenoid’s current is switched on, the ring jumps upward. Explain.
The rising current increases the flux through the ring, inducing a current in it. By Lenz’s law that current opposes the increase, so the ring’s field repels the solenoid’s field and the ring is thrown upward. If the ring has a slit in it, no current can flow and it does not jump — a classic lecture demonstration.
Does the resistance of the coil change the induced EMF? Does it change the induced current?
No to the first, yes to the second. EMF = −N·dΦ/dt depends only on the geometry and the motion. But I = EMF/R, so a lower resistance gives a larger current, more heating and a stronger braking force — which is why eddy-current brakes use thick copper rather than steel.
